Understanding and shifting power in biodiversity conservation.

Our approach ↗

Why
power
matters

Power shapes conservation decisions, relationships and possibilities for change.

Conserving biodiversity involves decisions about land, livelihoods, resources and the futures people want. Power shapes who can influence these decisions, whose knowledge and priorities count, and how conservation’s costs and benefits are distributed.

Power operates through relationships between people and organizations, but also through institutions, social norms and worldviews. These influence whose voices are heard, how conservation problems are understood and which approaches are considered possible.

Power can constrain action and reinforce inequalities. It can also emerge through people’s confidence and capacity to act, their alliances with others and the visions that guide them.

POWERSHIFT examines these interacting dimensions to understand how conservation actors experience, exercise and navigate power, and what this means for more just and effective conservation.

Framework

Our framework for understanding power

POWERSHIFT draws on the multidimensional framework developed through PowerBiodiv. The framework brings together four dimensions: arenas, expressions, spaces and levels of power.

It examines the interaction between power over, through which actors influence or control others and their possibilities, and transformative power, through which actors seek to change existing conditions. These can coexist: an initiative challenging one form of domination may reproduce another.

Arenas

How does power operate?

  • Visible powerFormal rules, institutions and observable decision-making processes, including policies and resource allocation.
  • Hidden powerControl over agendas and participation, including whose concerns reach decision-making spaces and whose are excluded.
  • Invisible powerBeliefs, ideologies, social norms and internalized assumptions that shape people’s understanding of themselves, others and the world.
  • Systemic powerThe deeply embedded structures and logics that organize social and economic life, including capitalism, colonialism, patriarchy and structural racism.

Expressions

How do people exercise power?

  • Power toThe capacity to act and pursue an objective.
  • Power withCollective strength developed through shared action, solidarity and alliances.
  • Power withinSelf-worth, self-knowledge and confidence, including recognition of one’s capacity to act.
  • Power forThe values, visions and purposes that inspire action and give it direction.

Spaces

Where does participation and influence happen?

  • Closed spacesDecisions are made within a restricted group.
  • Invited spacesPeople participate through an invitation from those convening a process.
  • Claimed spacesPeople create or assert spaces for participation and action themselves.

Levels

At what scale does power operate?

  • Power operates from households and local settings to national and international arenas. Processes at one level can enable or constrain action elsewhere.

These dimensions interact. The framework helps us examine their combinations and how they change across contexts and over time.

Research

Understanding power, aligning strategies and examining reflection

Our research follows four connected strands. We examine experiences of power at local intervention, organizational and network levels, explore future strategies with conservation actors, assess reflection throughout the project, and discuss the applicability of findings with policymakers and wider networks.

01

Understanding power in conservation

How do power dynamics shape conservation strategies and social and ecological outcomes?

We investigate experiences of power within local conservation interventions, conservation organizations and collaborative networks. Comparing these experiences will help identify recurring patterns and differences across contexts and levels.

Learn more: our methods

Local interventions

Fieldwork will combine in-depth interviews with documentary analysis. Interviews will explore how different actors perceive and experience power, including people involved in conservation activities and those affected by them.

Actor identification will pay particular attention to people who are marginalized or have limited influence, alongside more established institutional actors. Documentary research will examine the historical, cultural, policy and social-ecological context of each intervention.

Conservation organizations

Interviews with leaders of initiatives participating in the three networks will combine qualitative accounts with quantitative information. We will examine experiences of power, strategies used to navigate power imbalances and perceived consequences for conservation and people.

Social, economic and ecological indicators will support comparison across initiatives and the exploration of patterns between power dynamics, conservation practices and outcomes.

Conservation networks

Open-ended interviews with network leaders and key members will explore how power shapes collective organization, knowledge exchange, strategies and collective influence.

Analysis across these levels will examine whether common themes emerge and where experiences differ, without assuming that the same processes operate in every setting.

02

Aligning strategies for transformative change

How can conservation actors align their strategies to address power and strengthen their collective influence?

Through participatory futures workshops, we examine how conservation actors understand their agency, identify opportunities for power shifts and consider how their different strategies can work together.

Learn more: our methods

Workshops with each network will bring together three components.

Exploring power and ethics

Participants will engage with the power framework and findings from the first research strand. Interactive exercises, including role-play and case-based discussion, will explore how power is experienced in different settings.

Developing scenarios and pathways

Using the Disruptive Seeds approach, participants will explore preferable futures in which conservation power relations have changed. The proposed methods include an adapted Manoa Mash-up, combining Future Wheels and the Three Horizons framework.

These exercises will examine how emerging practices could develop, what interventions would be needed and where participants have the capacity to act or would need to work with others.

Mapping and aligning strategies

Participants will map strategies identified through the research using the Strategy Ecosystem approach, developed from social movement practice. This considers complementary strategies such as creating alternatives, shaping narratives, reforming institutions, resisting, organizing and networking.

Discussion will examine strengths, gaps and potential complementarities within each network, and how different strategies could contribute to structural and systemic change.

03

Examining reflection and changes in understanding

How does taking part in the project influence participants’ understanding of power and their approaches to it?

We assess how reflection through research interviews, futures workshops and dedicated activities affects participants’ understanding of power, their own positions and, potentially, their conservation practices. We also examine power within our research.

Learn more: our methods

Following changes over time

Questionnaires administered before research interviews, after the first research phase and after the futures workshops will document changes in participants’ understanding of power.

Together with material generated through the interviews and workshops, these will help examine how participants reflect on their assumptions, positions and privileges, and whether they identify implications for their work.

Reflection within local interventions

Tailored workshops will respond to challenges identified through the research and policy engagement. They will provide space for dialogue and critical reflection on power, including participants’ capacity to speak, listen and consider different perspectives.

Action Learning Sets

Selected practitioners from participating networks will take part in small, structured groups. These sessions will allow them to bring practical challenges, question assumptions with peers and reflect on attempts to address power in their work.

Reflection within the research team

Researchers will prepare individual statements on their perspectives, assumptions and positionality. A collective tanglegram will help make relationships and power within the project visible.

Participant observation and regular reflective journals will document the research process. Advisory board discussions will provide external perspectives on the team’s approach and emerging questions.

04

Examining relevance for policy and wider movements

Under what conditions can the project’s findings and approaches inform other conservation and governance contexts?

We engage policymakers, wider conservation networks and social movements to examine the relevance and applicability of the approaches explored through POWERSHIFT.

Learn more: our methods

Engagement in case-study countries

Initial meetings with policymakers will gather perspectives and identify relevant policy questions. Mid-project meetings will provide opportunities to discuss emerging findings and receive feedback.

Where relevant and desired, policymakers may also join tailored reflection workshops. End-of-project focus groups will examine the applicability of the strategies and recommendations emerging from the research.

Discussion in research-partner countries

Focus groups with policymakers will consider how findings relate to other national policy contexts. These discussions will examine potential barriers and the conditions needed for approaches to be adapted or implemented.

Exchanges with wider networks and movements

Dedicated strategy workshops will introduce the Strategy Ecosystem approach and discuss findings with other conservation networks and social movements.

Participants will consider their relevance to their own organizational structures, strategies and experiences of power, helping assess the possibilities and limits of applying the approaches more widely.

Our ambition

Theory
of
change

Investigating pathways towards more just and effective conservation

POWERSHIFT examines whether integrating social movement insights, strategic alignment and reflective practice can help conservation actors navigate and shift power relations.

Our Theory of Change identifies four intended outcomes:

  1. Improved understanding of how power influences conservation and social-ecological outcomes.
  2. Strengthened foresight and strategic alignment across conservation initiatives.
  3. A stronger culture of reflection and learning within conservation.
  4. Policies and practices that better reflect equitable and just conservation.

These pathways describe the project’s proposed contribution to transformative change. The research will examine the assumptions behind them and the conditions under which they may be realized.

Places

Where and with whom we work

Exploring power at different levels and in different contexts

POWERSHIFT examines power within local interventions, conservation organizations and collaborative networks.

The local case studies provide detailed accounts of particular conservation settings. Research across organizations and networks allows broader comparison. Together, these perspectives help us explore which themes recur across levels and which remain specific to a context.

Our local research involves HAkA in Indonesia, a platform on people and large carnivores in Italy, and WaSiMa in Tanzania. The participating networks are WFN’s Asian chapter, Maliasili and the regional platforms on people and large carnivores.

Forest conservation with HAkA

HAkA works to protect the Leuser Ecosystem in Aceh, Sumatra, combining conservation advocacy with forest monitoring and support for communities.

This context brings questions of forest governance, competing land uses, rights and participation into the study of power in conservation.

Learn more about this research context

The Leuser Ecosystem supports exceptional biodiversity and the lives and livelihoods of surrounding communities. Its protection involves competing priorities over forests, land and development.

HAkA’s work includes advocacy for ecosystem protection and support for community involvement in forest governance. Its wider activities include supporting women’s participation and leadership in forest protection.

POWERSHIFT will examine experiences of power within a selected intervention developed with HAkA, alongside the organizational and network research.

Negotiating coexistence with wolves

The Italian case focuses on a platform on people and large carnivores around Rome, where wolves are present in a landscape combining agricultural land, settlements and urban parks.

The platform brings together actors with different experiences, responsibilities and views of coexistence.

Learn more about this research context

Wolf presence in urban and peri-urban landscapes creates challenges for livestock keeping, wildlife management and everyday uses of shared space. It also carries strong cultural meanings and competing expectations about conservation.

The platform convenes actors including pastoralists, hunters, residents, tourism businesses, environmental organizations, veterinarians and public authorities to discuss coexistence and conflict mitigation.

POWERSHIFT will examine how participants experience power within this setting, how different perspectives enter discussions and how the surrounding institutional and social context shapes possibilities for action.

This case sits alongside research with the wider regional platforms on people and large carnivores.

Photo to come: village meeting with WaSiMa, western TanzaniaTanzania / WaSiMa

Community-led coexistence with WaSiMa

WaSiMa (Watu, Simba na Mazingira) works in western Tanzania to support coexistence between people and lions through community engagement, local governance and livelihood activities.

The case provides a setting for examining power within conservation efforts that seek to strengthen local participation and agency.

Learn more about this research context

Communities in western Tanzania experience challenges including livestock losses and crop damage, alongside wider questions about access to resources, livelihoods and relationships with conservation authorities.

WaSiMa’s approach includes community-developed bylaws, conflict mitigation and alternative income activities. Its work involves village institutions, local councils, conservation organizations and protected-area authorities.

POWERSHIFT will investigate how different actors understand and exercise power within this context, including how rules, responsibilities and opportunities to participate shape conservation practice.

The local research will be considered alongside findings from conservation organizations participating through Maliasili.

Network

Whitley Fund for Nature: Asian chapter

The Asian chapter brings together conservation leaders within WFN’s wider network. It offers a setting for examining experiences of power across varied conservation initiatives and for exploring strategic alignment through the project’s workshops.

Maliasili

Maliasili supports African conservation organizations through organizational development, leadership support and access to resources. Research with participating organizations will explore how power is experienced within their work and how they understand opportunities for collective influence.

Regional platforms on people and large carnivores

These platforms bring together actors concerned with coexistence between people and large carnivores in different European contexts. They provide settings for examining dialogue, differing interests and strategies for navigating conservation conflicts.
